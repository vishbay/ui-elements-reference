01 — Easing

What each curve communicates

Run them together and watch the difference in character, not speed — identical distance, identical duration. Choosing an easing curve is choosing what the movement means.

02 — Stagger

Interval is a design decision

Three intervals on the same eight elements. 30 ms reads as a single motion, 60 ms as a deliberate sequence, 140 ms as a page that is broken.

Light
Stagger interval
30 ms — reads as one motion
60 ms — deliberate, the usual answer
140 ms — too slow, feels broken
Total sequence length matters more than the interval. Keep the whole reveal under about 600 ms — past that, users start interacting mid-animation and the stagger becomes an obstacle rather than an introduction.

05 — Asymmetry

Enter and exit need different curves

The most common motion mistake is reusing one transition in both directions. Add and remove some filter chips to feel the difference.

Duration and easing by interaction

A starting table, not a law. The relationships matter more than the exact numbers — press faster than hover, close faster than open, distance scales duration.

Porting notes. Three relationships carry most of the craft: exits are faster than entrances, duration scales with distance travelled, and stagger intervals sit between 50 and 70 ms. Put every duration and curve in tokens so a single change adjusts the whole product's tempo. And measure the total length of any sequence — individually reasonable animations chained together routinely add up to a second and a half of waiting before a screen is usable.
